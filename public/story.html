Coptic Synaxis

History behind

A calendar you can trust in code — and readings you can open without fighting the books.

I write software, and I live with the Coptic Orthodox year the way many of us in the diaspora do: Sundays, fasts, feasts, and the quiet question that comes with them — what do we read today? I am not a liturgical scholar and not clergy. I just wanted that answer without juggling three books, a spreadsheet of dates, and a half-remembered rule about Lent.

The year is not a flat list of days. Annual, Great Lent, and Holy Fifty each bring their own katamaros. Vespers, matins, and liturgy each carry their own refs. Psalms are a special trap: the church book often shows Coptic numbering, while the English and Arabic texts most apps fetch use Hebrew numbering. Get that wrong and the passage looks empty for no obvious reason.

One date. One set of refs. One path from citation to bilingual text — reusable outside this site.

That is why the hard work is not locked inside the Synaxis screens. It lives in two open packages. coptic-readings takes a date and returns feast, tune, Coptic calendar fields, and the day’s reading references, picking the right seasonal book for you. bible-citation-text takes a citation string — including those refs — and resolves English and Arabic verse text. For Psalms, lookup uses the asset numbering; the Coptic form is kept only so the UI can show what the book says.

The app you see here is the thin layer on top: a year of seasons and feasts, today’s list, a bilingual viewer. If the interface changes tomorrow, the libraries should still answer the same two questions for anyone else building for the community. You can open the playground without installing anything.

What I hope for is simple. Less time reconstructing the calendar; more time with the readings — in prayer, study, or a parish tool of your own. If a feast is off, a citation is wrong, or a Psalm does not line up, tell me. Corrections make the shared data better for everyone.
